The AI Economic Transition

A more balanced perspective on the economic impacts from AI

Sub-sections:

The central take#

The institutional consensus is roughly that AI will be a productivity boost on the order of the IT revolution: meaningful, uneven, slow to diffuse, and absorbed by the economy without rupture. The Karger NBER survey (April 2026) is a fair summary of that view: even conditional on rapid AI progress, the median economist projects 3.5% GDP growth, an LFPR of 55%, and a top-decile wealth share of 80% by 2050.

The fat-tail counter-narrative argues the past is the wrong reference class entirely. AI is the first technology in modern economic history where the same input that creates productivity gains also eliminates the economic role of the workers it displaces.

The honest answer is that both are partially true. The median forecast is probably correct on aggregates and almost certainly wrong on volatility. The tail scenario is overconfident on feedback-loop coupling and probably correct on direction. The interesting question is not which scenario is right; it is which institutional adjustments need to happen regardless of which one lands, and whether they can happen fast enough to matter.

The economic transition will be determined less by what AI can do than by three coupled systems that were not built for it: a financial architecture priced on assumptions that will not survive, a tax base built on labor income in an increasingly capital-driven economy, and a safety net designed for cyclical rather than structural shocks. None of these will fail catastrophically on their own, but they are dangerously correlated.

Where the financial system breaks first#

Link one: SaaS pricing collapses toward marginal cost#

Agentic coding has already crossed the threshold where a competent engineer plus an LLM can replicate the core functionality of a mid-market SaaS product in weeks. This does not kill SaaS as a category — distribution, security, integration, and trust still have economic value — but it permanently changes the bargaining position in renewal negotiations. The procurement question shifts from "what's our discount this year" to "what's our build-versus-buy threshold," and that question alone compresses pricing power. We are seeing this in real time: long-tail horizontal SaaS is taking 20–40% renewal cuts where vendors used to clear 5% increases.

Link two: PE-backed software LBOs were priced for a world that no longer exists#

From 2018 through 2022, private equity took an enormous quantity of SaaS companies private at multiples of 10–20x ARR, financed with direct lending facilities structured around the assumption that ARR remains recurring. Vista, Thoma Bravo, Hellman & Friedman, Permira, Silver Lake, KKR and other major PE firms are exposed. Direct lenders (Blackstone, Apollo, Blue Owl, HPS, Ares) underwrote the debt against the same assumption.

If software pricing compresses, ARR is no longer the asset class it was underwritten as. The interesting tension is that public software multiples have already compressed to 5–8x EBITDA while PE-held software is still being marked closer to acquisition values.

Link three: Insurance is the contagion vector#

Over the last decade, the major alternative asset managers — Apollo, KKR, Brookfield, Blackstone, Ares — acquired or partnered with life insurance companies. Apollo bought Athene. Brookfield bought American Equity. KKR took Global Atlantic. The logic was straightforward: when you sell someone an annuity, you collect their money now and pay it back gradually over decades. That gives you a stable, long-duration pool of capital that you can invest. If you also happen to run a private credit business, you can invest the annuity money into loans your own firm originates, effectively earning a spread on the insurance side and management fees on the asset management side. Coupled with offshore reinsurance vehicles in Bermuda, this becomes a remarkably profitable machine.

The machine works if private credit is money-good. Defaults in the loan book pressure the insurer's capital ratios. State regulators like New York and Iowa have signaled concern and have tightened rules on privately-rated assets. The offshore reinsurance structures start to look like the monoline insurance complex of 2007.

The risk path runs from a fund managers portfolio, through a private credit fund, through a life insurer's balance sheet, through an offshore reinsurance vehicle, and lands in the monthly check of a retiree who has no idea their annuity provider was funding leveraged software buyouts.

Link four: When income structurally compresses, prime mortgage credit goes bad#

Knowledge-worker layoffs concentrated in the upper deciles of the income distribution have a wildly disproportionate effect on consumption. The top 10% of US earners drive roughly 50% of consumer spending; the top 20% drive 65%. A 2% decline in white-collar employment translates to something like a 3–4% hit to discretionary spending.

The mortgage layer is where this becomes financial. The US residential mortgage market is approximately $13 trillion, underwritten on the assumption that prime borrowers maintain roughly current incomes for the life of the loan. The borrowers in question are not subprime — 780 FICOs, 20% down, verified income, clean credit. The world simply changed after they were written.

The Fed can address financial conditions but cannot address income impairment. You can refinance someone into a lower payment, but if their income was structurally cut in half, they default eventually. This is the structural difference between every prior Fed intervention and the one that would be required here. Rate cuts and MBS purchases work when the problem is liquidity or interest-rate sensitivity. They do not work when the problem is significant income change.

Historical analogies and where they break#

2008 fits the insurance contagion architecture but not the asset quality#

The mechanical structure — correlated bets running through interconnected institutions, ratings agencies behind the curve, sponsor-aligned private structures hiding tail risk — rhymes with 2008. The difference is the asset quality. In 2008, the loans were bad on day one: NINJA loans, neg-am ARMs, subprime borrowers. In an AI-driven scenario, the loans are good on day one and become bad over time as the income assumptions underlying them dissolve.

2000 fits the equity dynamics but not the cash flow#

The dot-com analogy comes up because the surface features look familiar: a small number of technology companies trading at enormous multiples and massive capex on new infrastructure. The structural difference is that today's AI leaders have a real business underneath. Hyperscalers funding the AI infrastructure buildout (e.g., Microsoft, Google) are spending billions on data centers and chips out of operating cash flow. This is different from the speculative equity that funded the 2000 buildout.

There is a deeper concern: even if AI delivers real productivity gains, those gains may not route through the broader economy. They may stay concentrated as profits at NVIDIA, the hyperscalers and a handful of model labs. Acemoglu calls this "productivity without prosperity."

The Great Depression fits the labor dynamics but overshoots on policy capacity#

Pre-WWII inequality is the closest historical analog to where the rapid scenario projects we land. The top-10% wealth share peaked around 80–85% in the late 1920s and 1930s before the New Deal compressed it through tax policy, labor protections, and Social Security. Karger's economists projecting 80% by 2050 are projecting a return to roughly that pre-New-Deal level. The political economy that produced the New Deal was specific: a unified Democratic government, an executive willing to expand state capacity, a Supreme Court eventually willing to permit it, and a population willing to accept structural reforms. None of those conditions exist today.

The actual closest analog: the agricultural transition, 1900–1950#

In 1900, ~40% of US workers were in agriculture. By 1950, ~12%. By 2000, ~2%. The transition was driven by mechanization that made human agricultural labor uneconomic, and it played out over 50 years against a backdrop of massive aggregate productivity gains and structural political shifts. The Dust Bowl, the Great Migration, the populist movements of the 1890s through 1930s, and ultimately a series of farm support programs that persist to this day. All of these were the political-economy adjustments to a labor sector going from dominant to marginal. Aggregate growth was strong throughout. Median welfare improved. AI is doing to cognitive labor what mechanization did to agricultural labor. The transition takes decades, not years. Aggregate metrics improve while specific cohorts are devastated. The political response lags by 20–30 years and is partial when it arrives. The institutions that emerge afterward are unrecognizable from the ones that preceded the transition.

The frictions that buy us time#

Power is the binding constraint for frontier development, less so for deployment#

US data center electricity demand is projected to grow from approximately 4% of total US electricity consumption in 2024 to 9–12% by 2030, depending on whose model you trust. Grid interconnection queues in PJM, ERCOT, and MISO routinely stretch 4–7 years. New baseload generation — particularly nuclear, which is the only zero-carbon dispatchable option at scale — takes a decade from announcement to operational. Natural gas turbine capacity is sold out at major manufacturers through the late 2030s. This is the closest thing to a hard cap on AI deployment.

This friction is real, but it constrains training compute more than inference. Inference is increasingly running on edge devices and distilled models. The agentic deployment layer that drives white-collar displacement does not require new gigawatts. Power binds the frontier, not the diffusion.

Org change inertia inside large enterprises is the soft brake#

Fortune 500 companies are bad at organizational change even when individually rational. Internal politics, audit and compliance requirements, customer contract obligations, the fact that middle managers are voting on their own redundancy — all of these slow adoption beyond what the technology alone would suggest. Workday and ServiceNow have multi-year customer contracts that don't immediately renegotiate. Compliance frameworks (SOX, HIPAA, SOC2) require human accountability that is hard to delegate to AI.

Trust, accountability, and licensure are a narrower friction than they appear#

The conventional argument is that licensed professions – law, medicine, accounting, financial advice, engineering – are protected from AI because AI cannot hold a license or accept accountability. But, this protects less than it seems. Licensure protects a specific set of accountability functions: signing legal opinions, appearing in court, prescribing medication, stamping engineering drawings, signing audit reports. It does not protect the analytical labor underneath - contract review, legal research, financial modeling – which is the bulk of the work and the headcount.

The emerging pattern is the AI-native professional services firms: one licensed senior practitioner producing many multiples of prior volume. The cohort that gets compressed is the entry-level tier. The friction protects current senior practitioners, while accelerating the hollowing-out of the profession itself. Licensure protects the title, not the job.

Uneven exposure: the coast gets hit first#

Half of the US economy is in states, sectors, and demographic cohorts that move slower than the frontier. Rural, blue-collar, and physical-services work is significantly more insulated than the coastal knowledge-economy belt. This creates regional arbitrage and time. The pain is concentrated in tech-heavy ZIP codes and white-collar urban professions.

The tax architecture was built for the wrong economy#

If you accept that labor share of GDP continues to decline (it has fallen from 64% in 1974 to roughly 56% in 2024 and may compress further under AI), the existing tax architecture is structurally incompatible with the economy it is supposed to fund. Federal receipts are 70–80% income and payroll taxes. As labor's share of national income shrinks, federal revenues compress mechanically without any change in tax rates.

The CBO baseline already projects rising deficits driven by demographics and entitlement growth. Layer on AI-driven labor share compression and the gap widens substantially. The fiscal gap is not a far-future problem; it is a 5–10 year problem. The question is whether the tax architecture is restructured proactively or in crisis.

What the new architecture needs to look like#

Capital taxation has to do more work. Bastani and Waldenström (2024) make the optimal-tax case for this directly: as the labor tax base erodes, capital taxation becomes increasingly important to maintain redistributive capacity. The practical objection is mobility: capital is more mobile than labor, especially in a digital economy. The counter is that consumption is not mobile. A meaningful VAT, which the US is the only major OECD economy without, is the cleanest way to tax aggregate economic activity regardless of whether the income is labor or capital, domestic or offshore.

Compute taxation specifically has interesting properties. Karger's economists projected an AI Manhattan Project funded by a 0.7% VAT, but the more elegant structure is a tax on inference compute itself: the GPU-hours or token-throughput that constitutes the economic substitute for labor. Every other form of economic activity that displaces labor (industrial machinery, transportation, electricity) is taxed; AI compute is currently not. A modest tax on inference (say, $50/MWh on AI electricity above a threshold, or a per-token fee) creates a fiscal base that scales automatically with AI deployment, meaning the tax base grows precisely as the labor tax base shrinks. It is mechanically counter-cyclical in a way that traditional taxes are not.

The political objection is that compute taxation slows AI development and hands the lead to China. The economic counter is that a small compute tax (say, 1–2% of inference revenue) is dwarfed by the productivity gains AI delivers and is collected from the entities best positioned to pay. The geopolitical counter is that China's AI infrastructure is heavily state-funded already, which means the US can credibly tax its own without disadvantaging frontier development if the revenue is recycled into compute infrastructure or AI R&D.

Sovereign wealth as a structural claim on AI productivity#

The most ambitious version of the tax restructuring is a sovereign wealth fund that takes equity stakes in AI infrastructure — datacenter REITs, model labs, semiconductor capacity — funded by a small ongoing tax on AI revenue. This is not as radical as it sounds. Norway's Government Pension Fund Global owns 1.5% of every listed company in the world; the Alaska Permanent Fund pays a citizen dividend funded by oil revenue; Singapore's Temasek and GIC are essentially sovereign wealth funds funded by surpluses. A US AI sovereign wealth fund would be a way to give the public a direct claim on the productivity gains it is otherwise excluded from, without requiring direct nationalization or breakup of the model labs.

Anthropic itself has floated a version of this in the "Economic Futures" line of policy thinking, and Korinek (2024) has modeled it formally. It is the cleanest answer to the political-economy problem: how do you maintain political legitimacy in an economy where the productivity gains accrue overwhelmingly to capital, when 80% of the population is on the wrong side of that distribution?

What an economy not organized around labor looks like#

This is the part where the working perspective gets uncomfortable, because it requires acknowledging that the social contract built over the last 80 years is structurally tied to labor force participation. Healthcare is employer-tied. Retirement is 401(k)-based and assumes 40 years of continuous contribution. Social Security is funded by payroll taxes. Unemployment insurance is designed for cyclical job loss with quick re-employment. The dignity, social structure, and political legitimacy of American adulthood is organized around having a job. None of this works if 30–40% of working-age adults are not in the labor force.

Portable benefits as the foundational shift#

The cleanest structural fix is decoupling benefits from employment. Healthcare, retirement contributions, training credits, and disability coverage attached to the worker rather than the employer. This is not a new idea — it has been proposed for two decades and exists in partial form in some state-level frameworks for gig workers — but it has never been implemented at federal scale. The political coalition for it is awkward: it requires employer-side support (because it removes the administrative burden of benefits provision) and labor-side support (because it gives workers continuity), and historically these coalitions don't naturally align.

Singapore's Central Provident Fund is one functional template: a mandatory individual account funded by employer and employee contributions, used for retirement, healthcare, and housing, fully portable across employers and unemployment spells. Germany's Kurzarbeit short-time work program is another: government subsidies that maintain employer-employee relationships during downturns, preserving the human-capital match while distributing the income shock across the social insurance system. Neither is directly transferable to the US, but the design principles — portability, continuity across employment status, broad social risk pooling — are.

Wage insurance as the bridging mechanism#

Hyman, Kovak, and Leive (2024) showed that the US Trade Adjustment Assistance program shortens unemployment spells by 1.26 quarters on average, raises cumulative earnings by over $18,000 per displaced worker, and is self-financing once fiscal externalities are accounted for. TAA reaches roughly 50,000 workers per year. Scaling this to AI-displacement levels (potentially 1–2 million workers per year in the rapid scenario) is a 20–40x increase, which has never been politically feasible, but the underlying mechanism is exactly what AI-displaced workers need.

Wage insurance specifically — paying a fraction of the income gap when a displaced worker takes a lower-paying job, for 2–3 years — is the cleanest way to smooth the adjustment without creating disincentive-to-work problems. It maintains LFPR while compensating for the income loss, which is what UBI does not (UBI risks lowering LFPR while compensating for income loss).

The care economy as the destination labor market#

Karger's economists project care and service occupations rising to 57% of US employment under the rapid scenario — the highest in US history. Healthcare, eldercare, child care, education, mental health, and social services are the work that requires human presence, trust, and physical care, and they are the only sectors that systematically grow in every AI scenario. They are also chronically underpaid because most of the funding flows through Medicaid, Medicare, and out-of-pocket payments rather than enterprise budgets. If demand surges and supply tightens — which is exactly what an aging population plus AI displacement creates — wages should rise, but only if reimbursement structures allow it.

The infrastructure to support transitions — credentialing, training pipelines, reimbursement reform, immigration policy for direct care workers — is a tractable policy problem and also a tractable investment problem.

The political legitimacy question#

The hardest piece. If 30–40% of working-age adults are out of the labor force in 2050, and the productivity gains accrue to a top decile that owns the AI capital stack, the political economy is not stable in any historical reference class. The 1890s populist movement, the 1930s New Deal coalition, and the postwar labor settlement all required either trauma sufficient to break institutional inertia or political coalitions strong enough to push reform through. Neither condition is present in current US politics.

The more interesting question is whether the institutional response can come before the crisis rather than after it. The historical record on this is bad — major US institutional reforms almost always trail the underlying problem by 10–20 years — but the speed of AI diffusion compresses the window. A response that arrives in 2035 may be too late in a way that would have been fine in any prior technological transition.

Five questions that determine the path#

1. Does the bimodal diffusion hypothesis hold? If cognitive-task AI diffuses on a 5–7 year curve while physical AI diffuses on a 15–20 year curve, the labor market shock is concentrated and identifiable. If they collapse to similar timeframes, the displacement is broader and the policy response window narrower. The signal to watch: penetration rates of agentic AI in mid-market enterprise (above 50% by end of 2027 = fast diffusion confirmed).

2. Does the SaaS → PE → insurance contagion fire? The chain exists. Whether it activates depends on the speed of mark-down recognition in private credit. The signal to watch: Moody's/S&P downgrade activity in software-backed direct lending facilities, and the trajectory of secondary market trading in private credit. A 10%+ default rate in software LBO debt by 2027 is the threshold where contagion becomes likely.

3. What does the political economy do as the labor tax base erodes? Federal receipts running 8–12% below CBO baseline by 2027 forces a tax architecture conversation. Whether that conversation produces a VAT, compute taxation, sovereign wealth fund — or just deeper deficits and inflation — is the central political-economy variable. The signal to watch: any bipartisan working group on tax reform that survives past the 2026 midterms.

4. Where does new job creation actually emerge? The historical pattern is that displaced labor flows into new categories that were not predictable in advance. Care economy, AI-complement roles, trades, and physical services are the reasonable a priori candidates. The signal to watch: BLS occupational employment data for sectors growing faster than population. A meaningful surge in care economy roles with rising wages would indicate the transition is finding its place.

5. Does the US institutional response come before or after the crisis? This is the highest-variance question and the one where individual contributions actually matter. The signal to watch: federal-level proposals on portable benefits, wage insurance scaling, or sovereign wealth concepts gaining traction in policy circles before the labor market visibly cracks. If the conversation is still purely academic in 2027, the response is coming after the crisis.

Bottom line#

Probability-weighted, this is not doomsday. The most likely path is a 7–15 year period of regional and demographic-specific pain, a partial and inadequate institutional response, structural reorganization of specific industries (SaaS pricing collapse and PE/private-credit stress are happening regardless of which scenario you believe), and a gradual remaking of the tax-and-transfer system under fiscal pressure. The aggregates probably look closer to Karger's median. The volatility around those aggregates is what matters.

The tail risk is whether the feedback loops fire faster than the policy response — mortgage stress in tech ZIP codes, PE/insurance contagion, regional municipal finance crises, sustained political instability.

The institutional adjustment — tax architecture, portable benefits, wage insurance, sovereign wealth, care economy infrastructure — needs to happen regardless of which scenario lands. The case for it is strongest if you take the median forecast seriously, because even Karger's economists project a top-decile wealth share at 80% by 2050, which is a level the US has historically only sustained through major political-economy reform. If you take the tail seriously, the case becomes urgent rather than important.

Being directionally right matters more than being precisely right. The precise timing of when SaaS multiples bottom, when the first major PE software default hits, when mortgage delinquencies spike in San Francisco, or when Congress finally passes wage insurance legislation — none of these are predictable in advance. The direction is. The trade is to build for the end state of the transition rather than try to time the path through it.

The window for proactive institutional response is open, just narrower than most people assume.
